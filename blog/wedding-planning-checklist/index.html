Wedding Planning Checklist: 12 Months to the Big Day

Planning a wedding in the Philippines often involves two families, a church or civil ceremony, and a long list of people you love. This checklist is a gentle guide—not a strict rulebook. Move things earlier or later as it suits your date, your budget, and your pace.

12 to 9 months before

Start with the conversations that shape everything else. Sit down with your families, share the kind of day you hope to have, and be honest about what you can comfortably spend. It is easier to plan when everyone hears the same story early.

  • Choose a wedding date, or at least a month and a few backup dates. If you are looking at the rainy months, think about a solid indoor option or a covered plan B.
  • Decide on the ceremony type: church, garden, or civil. Each has its own requirements and timeline, so it helps to know this before you book anything else.
  • Begin venue visits. Go in person if you can, at a time of day close to your ceremony or reception, and walk the spaces the way your guests would.
  • Start a shared notebook or folder for quotes, contacts, and decisions so nothing lives only in a message thread.
  • Sketch a first guest list together. You can trim it later, but a rough number helps when you talk to venues and caterers.

8 to 6 months before

This is a good window to lock in the people and suppliers who get booked out on popular weekends.

  • Confirm your venue and put the agreement in writing. Read the inclusions, overtime rules, and payment schedule carefully.
  • If you are marrying in church, ask about pre-Cana or other required seminars, document lists, and how far in advance you should reserve the time.
  • Choose your principal sponsors (ninong and ninang), secondary sponsors, and the rest of the entourage. Give people time to say yes and to prepare.
  • Book a photographer and videographer, and talk through the moments that matter most to you—family portraits, the ceremony, the unscripted parts of the reception.
  • Look into attire for the couple and, if you are providing them, for the entourage. Fitting schedules fill up too.
  • If you want a particular caterer, florist, stylist, or host, ask the venue whether they work with outside suppliers or a preferred list.

5 to 4 months before

  • Work on invitations, whether printed, digital, or both. Leave time for proofreading names, especially titles and family listings.
  • Plan the menu and, if offered, attend a tasting. Note allergies and a few options for children or older guests.
  • Think about guest comfort: parking, restrooms, shade or cooling, and how people will travel from the ceremony to the reception.
  • Start the civil requirements for your marriage license with your local civil registrar. Rules can differ by city or municipality, so ask them directly for the current list and waiting period.
  • If you want prenup photos, choose a date that does not compete with fittings, seminars, or a busy work week.

3 to 2 months before

  • Send invitations and keep a simple RSVP list. Follow up kindly with people who have not replied.
  • Finalize the program with your coordinator or host: processional, rites, reception flow, and speeches, so the day does not feel rushed.
  • Confirm styling, flowers, cake, souvenirs, and music. Share a floor plan or reference photos so everyone is picturing the same setup.
  • Book hair and makeup trials, including a plan for touch-ups later in the day.
  • Arrange transportation for the couple, immediate family, and, if needed, seniors who should not have to find their own way.
  • Prepare gifts or tokens for sponsors and the entourage, and a short note if you want to thank them in person.

One month before

  • Confirm final guest count with the venue and caterer by their deadline.
  • Double-check permits, payments, and remaining balances. Keep receipts in one place.
  • Share a shot list and a family grouping list with your photographer so portraits move quickly.
  • Break in your shoes and do a full attire rehearsal, including accessories.
  • Write vows or personal notes if you plan to share them. It is fine if they are short and in the language you actually speak together.
  • Assign a point person—often a coordinator, sibling, or close friend—who can answer questions on the day so you do not have to.

The week of the wedding

  • Reconfirm arrival times with every supplier. Share a simple timeline and a map or pin for those who have not visited.
  • Pack a small emergency kit: sewing kit, fashion tape, medicine, snacks, a phone charger, and copies of important documents.
  • Prepare final payments and tips in labeled envelopes, if that is how you planned to handle them.
  • Eat regular meals and try to sleep. A calm couple makes the whole room feel calmer.
  • On the day itself, give yourselves a few private minutes before guests arrive. The checklist can wait; this part of the day will not come again.

If a step above does not apply to your celebration, skip it. The goal is not a perfect plan. It is a day that feels like you, with enough structure that you can be present with the people who came for you.
